# How to Keep Track of Your Pet's Vaccinations (and Other Health Records)

"When was your dog's last rabies shot?"

If that question sends you scrolling through old emails, digging through a drawer of receipts, or calling your vet's front desk, you're not alone. Many pet owners don't think about vaccine records until a boarding facility, groomer, daycare, or new vet asks for them, often on short notice.

A simple record-keeping system saves time, reduces stress, and can even help your vet provide better care. Here's how to keep track of your pet's vaccinations and other important health information.

## Why keeping pet health records matters

**Boarding, daycare, and grooming.** Most facilities require proof of current vaccinations. Requirements vary, but rabies is commonly required, and many facilities also ask about other vaccines depending on the species and services.

**Travel.** Traveling across state lines or internationally can require health certificates and proof of rabies vaccination. International travel often has strict timing requirements, so records need to be accurate.

**Legal requirements.** Rabies vaccination for dogs is required by law in most U.S. states, and many localities also require it for cats. Local licensing often requires proof of rabies vaccination.

**Emergencies.** If your pet needs emergency care, especially at an unfamiliar clinic, having vaccination history, medications, allergies, and past conditions on hand can help the veterinary team.

**Switching vets or moving.** Records help your new vet pick up where the old one left off.

**Multiple pets.** With two or more pets, it's easy to mix up who had what, and when.

## Understanding your pet's vaccinations

Vaccination recommendations are individualized, and your veterinarian will recommend a schedule based on your pet's age, health, lifestyle, and local risks. In general, vaccines are often grouped into two categories:

**Core vaccines** are recommended for most pets. For dogs, these commonly include rabies, distemper, parvovirus, and adenovirus (hepatitis), often combined as a "DHPP" or similar vaccine. For cats, core vaccines commonly include rabies and "FVRCP" (feline viral rhinotracheitis, calicivirus, and panleukopenia).

**Non-core (lifestyle) vaccines** are recommended based on individual risk. For dogs, these may include vaccines for Bordetella (kennel cough), leptospirosis, Lyme disease, and canine influenza. For cats, feline leukemia (FeLV) vaccination is often recommended for kittens and for cats at risk of exposure.

Puppies and kittens typically receive a series of vaccines several weeks apart in their first months, followed by boosters. Adult pets receive boosters at intervals your vet recommends, which can be one year or three years depending on the vaccine, product, and local regulations.

That's a lot of dates to remember, and exactly why a tracking system helps.

## What to record for each vaccination

For every vaccine, try to capture:

- **Vaccine name** (for example, rabies, DHPP, FVRCP, Bordetella)
- **Date given**
- **Next due date**
- **Veterinarian or clinic name**
- **Manufacturer and lot number** (often printed on the certificate or sticker)
- **Rabies tag number**, if applicable
- **Any reactions** your pet had afterward

The rabies certificate itself is an official document. Keep the original somewhere safe and bring a copy (or a photo) when needed.

## Beyond vaccines: other records worth keeping

A complete health record gives a fuller picture of your pet's health over time. Consider tracking:

- **Pet profile:** Name, breed, birth or adoption date, color and markings, microchip number and registry, insurance policy details
- **Vet visits:** Date, reason for visit, findings, recommendations, and follow-up dates
- **Medications:** Name, dose, frequency, start and end dates, and who prescribed it
- **Preventives:** Flea, tick, and heartworm prevention and the dates given
- **Weight:** Regular weigh-ins help you and your vet spot gradual changes
- **Allergies and sensitivities:** Foods, medications, or vaccine reactions
- **Grooming:** Baths, nail trims, ear cleaning, and dental care
- **Emergency contacts:** Your regular vet, nearest emergency clinic, and the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center (888-426-4435, a fee may apply)

## Choosing a tracking method

There's no single right way to keep records. The best system is the one you'll actually use.

### Option 1: Your vet's client portal

Many clinics offer online portals or apps with vaccine histories and reminders. These are convenient, but they only include care given at that clinic, and access may disappear if you switch vets.

### Option 2: Digital folders

Create a folder for each pet (in cloud storage, for example) and save PDFs of certificates, invoices, and lab results. Name files with the date and description so they're easy to find. Add due dates to your phone calendar with reminders a couple of weeks in advance.

### Option 3: A printed record book

A physical binder or record book is easy to grab on the way to the vet, doesn't require logging in, and is simple for pet sitters and family members to use. Many owners pair it with calendar reminders on their phone.

## Tips for staying on top of it

1. **Update records right after each vet visit.** It only takes two minutes while details are fresh. Ask the front desk for a printed or emailed summary.
2. **Set reminders for due dates.** Schedule reminders a few weeks ahead so you have time to book an appointment.
3. **Keep a photo of the rabies certificate on your phone.** Handy for last-minute boarding or daycare requests.
4. **Request records from previous vets or shelters.** If you adopted your pet or recently moved, ask for a complete history.
5. **Review records once a year.** A yearly check-in, maybe on your pet's birthday or adoption day, helps you catch anything that's overdue.
6. **Share with caregivers.** Leave a copy of key information with pet sitters and family members.

## New to pet parenthood?

If you just brought home a puppy or kitten, start your records on day one. Bring any paperwork from the breeder or shelter to your first vet visit so your veterinarian can plan the rest of the vaccine series.
