# How to Potty Train a Puppy in 30 Days: A Week-by-Week Plan

Potty training is usually the first big project for a new puppy owner, and it can feel endless when you're mopping up your third accident before lunch. The good news: most puppies learn the basics quickly when you give them a consistent routine, lots of chances to get it right, and a reward every time they do.

This guide walks you through exactly how to potty train a puppy over 30 days. Every puppy is different, and some will need more time (especially very young puppies or small breeds with tiny bladders), but a month is a realistic window to build solid habits.

## Why potty training works (and why it sometimes doesn't)

Puppies aren't born knowing that indoors is off-limits. They learn by repetition: when they go potty in the right spot and something good happens, they're more likely to go there again. When they have accidents indoors without anyone noticing, they learn that indoors works too.

So the whole game comes down to two ideas:

1. **Maximize successes.** Take your puppy outside often, especially at predictable times, and reward them the moment they finish.
2. **Minimize unsupervised accidents.** Every accident you don't see is a rehearsal of the wrong habit.

Punishment doesn't fit anywhere in this equation. Scolding or rubbing a puppy's nose in a mess tends to teach them to hide when they need to go, which makes training harder.

## Before you start: set up for success

A little prep makes the next 30 days much smoother.

- **Pick a potty spot.** Choose one area of your yard or a nearby patch of grass. Familiar smells help puppies understand what that spot is for.
- **Choose a cue word.** Something short like "go potty" or "be quick." Say it calmly as your puppy starts to go, and eventually it becomes a helpful prompt.
- **Set up a crate or small pen.** Most dogs avoid soiling where they sleep, so a properly sized crate (big enough to stand, turn around, and lie down, but not much bigger) helps your puppy learn to hold it for short periods.
- **Stock high-value treats.** Tiny, soft treats your puppy loves. Keep a pouch by the door.
- **Get an enzyme cleaner.** Regular cleaners may leave odors only your puppy can smell. Enzyme cleaners break those down so your puppy isn't drawn back to the same spot.

## How often does a puppy need to go out?

A common rule of thumb is that puppies can hold their bladder for roughly one hour per month of age, plus one, during the day. That means a 2-month-old puppy may manage about 2 to 3 hours, and a 4-month-old about 4 to 5 hours. It's an estimate, not a guarantee, and overnight capacity is usually a bit better once they're settled.

Beyond the clock, take your puppy out:

- First thing in the morning
- Right after waking from any nap
- Within 10 to 20 minutes after eating or drinking
- During and after play sessions
- Before bedtime
- Any time you see sniffing, circling, or sudden wandering away

## The 30-day puppy potty training plan

### Week 1: Build the routine

Your goal this week is simple: lots of trips outside and lots of rewards.

- **Take your puppy out on a schedule,** roughly every 1 to 2 hours during the day, plus all the trigger times above.
- **Go out with them,** on leash, straight to the potty spot. Stand quietly and give them a few minutes.
- **Reward immediately.** The instant they finish, praise and give a treat right there outside, not back in the house. Timing matters: you're rewarding the act, in that place.
- **No success? Back inside, and supervise closely** or place them in their crate for 10 to 15 minutes, then try again.
- **Feed meals at consistent times** so potty times become more predictable.
- **Keep a log.** Write down when your puppy eats, drinks, sleeps, and goes. After a few days, you'll see patterns that make timing much easier.

### Week 2: Tighten supervision and read the signals

By now you should start noticing your puppy's "I need to go" signals.

- **Use the umbilical method or a baby gate** to keep your puppy in sight whenever they're loose in the house. A leash clipped to your belt works surprisingly well.
- **Watch for pre-potty behavior:** sniffing the floor, circling, heading toward a corner, or restlessness.
- **Start using your cue word** just as your puppy begins to go.
- **Keep rewarding every success.** Don't phase out treats too early.
- **Look at your log.** Are accidents happening at the same time of day? Add a potty trip before that time.

### Week 3: Stretch the time between trips (gradually)

If accidents have become rare, you can begin to extend intervals.

- **Add 15 to 30 minutes** between scheduled trips, keeping all the trigger times (waking, eating, play).
- **Introduce a door signal.** Some owners hang a bell at the door and ring it on the way out each time. Over time, many puppies learn to ring it themselves.
- **Give a little more freedom,** one room at a time, but only after your puppy has been outside and emptied out.
- **If accidents increase, step back** to the previous schedule for a few days. That's normal, not failure.

### Week 4: Build reliability

This week is about consistency in new situations.

- **Practice at different times and places,** such as a friend's yard or a new walking route, so your puppy learns that "potty outside" applies everywhere.
- **Keep rewards going,** though you can start mixing in praise and play some of the time.
- **Stay consistent overnight.** Young puppies may still need one nighttime trip. Keep it boring: out, potty, reward, back to bed.
- **Celebrate progress.** Track accident-free days so you can see how far you've come.

## What to do when accidents happen

They will happen. Here's how to handle them calmly:

- **If you catch your puppy mid-accident,** calmly interrupt with a neutral sound, scoop them up or lead them outside, and reward if they finish there.
- **If you find an accident afterward,** just clean it up. Your puppy won't connect a scolding to something they did even a few minutes ago.
- **Clean thoroughly with an enzyme cleaner.**
- **Ask yourself what happened.** Too long between trips? Too much freedom? Missed signals? Adjust the plan, not the puppy.

## Common potty training mistakes

- **Giving freedom too soon.** A puppy with the run of the house has many chances to practice going indoors.
- **Rewarding too late.** A treat given back inside rewards coming inside, not going potty.
- **Inconsistent schedules.** Irregular meals and random potty times make patterns hard to learn.
- **Using the crate too long.** Crates are helpful, but a puppy left too long will be forced to go, which can undo their natural instinct to keep their space clean.
- **Expecting a young puppy to "tell" you.** Signaling comes with time. Early on, it's your job to anticipate.

## When to call your vet

Some potty problems aren't about training at all. Contact your veterinarian if your puppy:

- Suddenly starts having accidents after doing well
- Urinates very frequently, strains, or seems uncomfortable
- Has blood in their urine or stool
- Drinks noticeably more water than usual
- Has diarrhea that lasts more than a day or comes with vomiting or lethargy

Urinary tract infections, parasites, and other health issues can look like training setbacks.

## The bottom line

Potty training a puppy in 30 days comes down to a predictable routine, close supervision, and rewarding the right behavior every single time. Keep a log, adjust as you go, and remember that small setbacks are part of the process.
