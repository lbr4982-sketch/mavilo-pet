# New Puppy Checklist: What to Buy Before You Bring Your Puppy Home

You've picked the name, maybe even the collar color. Now it's time to get your home ready. A good new puppy checklist keeps you from forgetting the essentials and from filling a cart with things your puppy won't need for months (or ever).

Below you'll find everything to buy before your puppy arrives, what can wait, how to puppy-proof your home, and a quick plan for the first week.

## The essentials: buy these before day one

### Food and feeding

- **Puppy food.** Ask the breeder or shelter what your puppy is currently eating and buy a bag of the same food. Sudden diet changes can upset a puppy's stomach. If you want to switch foods later, do it gradually over about a week, and talk with your vet about a complete and balanced diet appropriate for your puppy's expected adult size.
- **Food and water bowls.** Stainless steel or ceramic bowls are durable and easy to clean.
- **Treats.** Small, soft training treats you can break into tiny pieces. You'll use a lot of them.
- **Measuring cup.** Helps you feed consistent portions, which makes potty timing and weight management easier.

### Sleeping and containment

- **Crate.** Choose one that will fit your puppy as an adult, with a divider panel so you can adjust the space as they grow. The space should be just big enough to stand, turn around, and lie down comfortably.
- **Bedding.** A washable crate pad or blanket. Expect some chewing, so start with something inexpensive.
- **Baby gates or an exercise pen.** These let you limit your puppy to puppy-proofed areas while you're supervising.

### Walking and ID

- **Collar or harness.** Adjustable and properly fitted: you should be able to slip two fingers underneath. Many owners prefer a harness for walks with young puppies.
- **ID tag.** Include your phone number. Your puppy should wear it from day one.
- **Leash.** A standard 4 to 6 foot leash is ideal for training. Retractable leashes make it harder to teach loose-leash walking.
- **Microchip.** Many breeders and shelters microchip before adoption. If yours hasn't, ask your vet, and make sure the chip is registered with your current contact information.

### Potty training supplies

- **Enzyme cleaner.** Non-negotiable. It breaks down odors that may attract your puppy back to the same spot.
- **Poop bags** and a dispenser that clips to the leash.
- **Paper towels.** More than you think.
- **Puppy pads (optional).** Useful for some apartment situations, but if your goal is outdoor potty training, relying on pads can slow things down.

### Chewing and play

Puppies explore with their mouths, especially during teething. Give them appropriate options:

- **A few chew toys** of different textures (rubber, nylon, and soft)
- **A stuffable food toy** for enrichment and crate time
- **A tug toy and a ball** for interactive play

Rotate toys every few days to keep them interesting. Always choose toys sized appropriately so they can't be swallowed, and supervise with anything that can be torn apart.

### Grooming basics

- **A brush suited to your puppy's coat**
- **Nail clippers or a grinder**
- **Puppy-safe shampoo**
- **A dog toothbrush and dog toothpaste** (never human toothpaste)

Start gentle handling of paws, ears, and mouth early, even if you're not trimming or brushing yet. It pays off for years.

## Nice-to-haves that can wait

It's easy to overbuy. These can usually wait until you know your puppy's personality and size:

- Expensive orthopedic beds (they may get chewed)
- A large wardrobe of outfits
- Automatic feeders and water fountains
- Dozens of toys
- Specialty training tools

Buy basics first, then add what your actual puppy needs.

## How to puppy-proof your home

Get down to puppy eye level and look around. Anything within reach will be investigated.

**Living areas**
- Secure or cover electrical cords and chargers
- Put away shoes, socks, kids' toys, and remote controls
- Move houseplants out of reach; many common plants are toxic to pets. The ASPCA maintains a searchable list of toxic and non-toxic plants.

**Kitchen**
- Use trash cans with locking lids
- Keep foods that are dangerous to dogs out of reach, including chocolate, grapes and raisins, onions and garlic, xylitol (found in some sugar-free gum and products), and alcohol
- Store cleaning products behind closed or latched cabinets

**Bathroom**
- Keep medications, vitamins, and personal care products put away
- Close toilet lids

**Garage and yard**
- Store antifreeze, fertilizers, pesticides, and rodent bait securely
- Check fences for gaps and gates for secure latches

Keep the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center number (888-426-4435, a fee may apply) and your nearest emergency vet clinic's number somewhere easy to find.

## Your first-week plan

Supplies are only half the job. The first week sets up routines your puppy will rely on.

**Day 1:** Keep it quiet. Show your puppy their potty spot first, then let them explore a small, puppy-proofed area. Limit visitors. Introduce the crate with treats and a chew toy, and keep the door open at first.

**Days 2 to 3:** Establish a consistent schedule for meals, potty trips, naps, and play. Puppies sleep a lot, often 18 hours or more a day, so protect nap time.

**Days 4 to 7:** Start short training sessions (a few minutes at a time) for name recognition and simple cues. Begin gentle handling practice. Schedule your first vet visit if you haven't already.

**Within the first week or two:** Visit your veterinarian for a checkup, a vaccination schedule, and parasite prevention recommendations. Bring any records from the breeder or shelter.

## Budgeting for your new puppy

Setting up for a puppy often costs a few hundred dollars in one-time supplies, plus ongoing monthly costs for food, treats, preventives, and toys. Veterinary care in the first year (vaccines, checkups, spay/neuter) adds more. Costs vary widely by location and dog size, so writing out your own numbers is the best way to avoid surprises. Also consider pet insurance or a dedicated savings fund for unexpected vet bills.

## Quick recap: the new puppy checklist

- Puppy food (same as current), bowls, treats
- Crate, bedding, gates or pen
- Collar or harness, ID tag, 4 to 6 foot leash, registered microchip
- Enzyme cleaner, poop bags, paper towels
- Chew toys, a food puzzle, tug and ball
- Brush, nail clippers, shampoo, dog toothbrush and toothpaste
- Puppy-proofed home and emergency numbers posted
- First vet appointment booked

Welcome to puppy parenthood. It's messy, tiring, and completely worth it.
