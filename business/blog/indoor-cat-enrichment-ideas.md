# Indoor Cat Enrichment Ideas: 15 Easy Ways to Keep Your Cat Happy

Keeping cats indoors protects them from traffic, predators, and many diseases, and it protects local wildlife too. But life indoors can get repetitive. A cat who spends the day sleeping and the night racing through the hallway, knocking things off counters, or meowing at 4 a.m. is often telling you something: they need more to do.

Enrichment is simply giving your cat opportunities to use their natural behaviors (hunting, climbing, scratching, exploring, and hiding) in safe ways. Here are 15 indoor cat enrichment ideas you can start today, most of them free or close to it.

## Why enrichment matters for indoor cats

Cats are natural predators. In the wild, a cat spends a big part of the day stalking, chasing, and catching small prey, often many small meals a day. Indoors, food shows up in a bowl and nothing needs to be hunted.

Without outlets for these instincts, some cats develop behaviors owners find frustrating: nighttime zoomies, attacking ankles, overgrooming, overeating, or scratching furniture. Enrichment gives that energy somewhere to go and can support both physical and mental well-being.

## Hunting and play

### 1. Use the hunt, catch, eat sequence

The most satisfying play mimics a real hunt. Use a wand toy and move it like prey: skittering along the floor, hiding behind furniture, pausing, then darting away. Let your cat stalk, pounce, and catch it. End the session by letting them "win" and then offering a small meal or treat. Many cats settle down contentedly after.

### 2. Schedule short daily play sessions

Two or three sessions of about 10 to 15 minutes a day works well for many cats. Evening play, before your bedtime, can help reduce early-morning wake-ups.

### 3. Rotate toys

Cats get bored of the same toys. Keep most toys put away and swap a few out every few days, so each one feels new again.

### 4. Try a cardboard box ambush

Cut a couple of holes in a cardboard box and poke a wand toy in and out of the holes. Many cats find this irresistible.

### 5. Add a crinkle tunnel or paper bag

Paper bags (with handles cut off for safety) and tunnels create hiding and pouncing opportunities. Never leave plastic bags out.

## Food-based enrichment

### 6. Use food puzzles

Instead of a bowl, serve part of your cat's daily food in a puzzle feeder that requires batting, rolling, or reaching. Start with an easy puzzle so your cat doesn't give up.

### 7. Make a DIY puzzle feeder

Cut a few holes slightly larger than a piece of kibble in an empty cardboard tube, fold the ends closed, and add some kibble. Your cat bats it around to release the food. An empty egg carton with treats tucked into the cups works too.

### 8. Play hide and seek with treats

Hide small portions of food or treats around a room: on a low shelf, behind a chair leg, inside a box. This encourages foraging and exploration. Start with easy spots.

### 9. Split meals into smaller portions

Several small meals throughout the day are closer to how cats naturally eat. Timed feeders or scattered portions can help, especially if you're away during the day. Be sure the total amount stays within your cat's daily food allowance, and account for treats.

## Territory and environment

### 10. Go vertical

Cats feel safer and more confident with height. Cat trees, wall shelves, or even a cleared-off bookshelf give your cat places to climb, perch, and survey their territory. In multi-cat homes, extra vertical space can help reduce tension.

### 11. Provide a window seat

A perch by a window is like cat TV. A bird feeder outside the window (placed with bird safety in mind) can add even more interest.

### 12. Offer good scratching options

Scratching is a normal behavior that helps cats maintain claws, stretch, and mark territory. Offer both vertical posts (tall and sturdy enough for a full stretch) and horizontal cardboard scratchers. Place them near sleeping areas and in visible spots, not tucked in a corner.

### 13. Create hiding spots

Every cat needs places to retreat. Boxes, covered beds, and cleared-off spaces under furniture give your cat somewhere safe to decompress.

## Senses and novelty

### 14. Try scent enrichment

Cats experience much of their world through smell. Some cats respond to catnip, and some that don't respond to catnip may respond to silvervine or valerian. You can also bring in safe new smells: a cardboard box from the store, a clean sock, or a few leaves from outside (from pesticide-free areas, and never toxic plants such as lilies, which are extremely dangerous to cats).

### 15. Teach a trick with clicker training

Yes, cats can learn tricks. Clicker training or a marker word with small treats can teach your cat to sit, high-five, or come when called. Short training sessions are great mental exercise and build your bond.

## Safety notes

- **Put away string toys after play.** Strings, ribbons, hair ties, and yarn can be dangerous if swallowed.
- **Check toys for loose parts,** like feathers, bells, or eyes, that could come off.
- **Supervise with new DIY toys** until you know how your cat interacts with them.
- **Keep toxic plants out of the home.** Lilies are especially dangerous to cats. The ASPCA has a searchable list of plants that are toxic to cats.
- **Secure window screens** before encouraging window perching.

## Building an enrichment routine

The most effective enrichment is the kind that happens consistently. A simple weekly rhythm might look like this:

- **Daily:** Two short interactive play sessions, one meal in a puzzle feeder
- **Every few days:** Rotate toys and add a new box or bag
- **Weekly:** Try a new idea, such as a scent game, a treat hunt, or a new perch

Pay attention to what your cat loves. Some cats are chasers, some are pouncers, and some prefer puzzles to play. Keeping notes helps you figure out their favorites.

## When to talk to your vet

Sudden changes in behavior, such as hiding more than usual, aggression, reduced appetite, changes in litter box habits, excessive grooming, or unusual lethargy, can signal pain or illness rather than boredom. If you notice these changes, schedule a vet visit before assuming enrichment is the answer.

## Final thoughts

Your indoor cat doesn't need an expensive setup to be happy. A cardboard box, a wand toy, and a few minutes of your time each day go a long way. Start with one or two ideas from this list, watch how your cat responds, and build from there.
